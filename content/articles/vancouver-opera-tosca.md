title = "Grand visuals & lowered stakes in Vancouver Opera's Tosca"
+++
Vancouver Opera opened its 2026–27 season with Puccini's _Tosca_, a work that depends on danger, passion, and an almost unbearable sense of momentum. This new co-production with Pacific Opera Victoria was visually sumptuous and musically impressive, but dramatically it never quite generated the tension that should make _Tosca_ such a gripping evening in the theatre.

Visually, the production was beautiful. Scott Reid's set and projection design created an imposing and convincing Rome, providing the production with a sense of scale without overwhelming the performers. Kevin Knight's costumes, supplied by the Canadian Opera Company, were equally impressive: richly detailed, elegant, perfect for the period, and entirely at home within the production’s traditional setting. Together, the sets and costumes gave the evening a handsome visual coherence and a strong sense of period.

The Vancouver Opera Chorus was another highlight. Under Chorus Director Tina Chang, the ensemble produced a powerful, full-bodied sound, particularly in the great Act I _Te Deum_. The scene demands grandeur and overwhelming force, and the chorus delivered both, filling the theatre with some of the evening's most exciting music.

The comprimario roles were uniformly excellent, with Peter Monaghan's Sacristan a particular delight. Monaghan brought genuine comic timing, warmth, and an effortless stage presence to the role, finding the humour without ever turning the character into caricature. His performance was funny, engaging, and wonderfully specific, making the Sacristan memorable despite his relatively brief time onstage. Neil Craighead was equally compelling as Cesare Angelotti, while Callum Alden's Spoletta and Mihnea Nitu's Sciarrone were both sharply characterized and made much of their smaller roles.

The central pairing of David Pomeroy's Cavaradossi and Joyce El-Khoury's Tosca was less successful. Both are accomplished singers, but their voices inhabit very different worlds. Pomeroy's tenor is bright and forward, with a broad vibrato. El-Khoury, by contrast, brought a considerably darker, more covered sound to Tosca. Individually, each had compelling moments, but together their contrasting vocal colours did not blend particularly naturally.

More importantly for an opera driven by obsessive love, the relationship rarely felt combustible. There was little sense of physical or emotional chemistry between Tosca and Cavaradossi, making it difficult to believe in the consuming passion that motivates so much of what follows. Puccini gives the pair music of extraordinary intimacy and urgency.

That problem continued with Gregory Dahl's Scarpia. Dahl sang with authority, but the characterization was surprisingly restrained. Rather than the predatory, terrifying figure who is arguably the greatest villain in opera, this Scarpia came across more as a self-serving bureaucrat. There is certainly an interesting argument to be made for presenting evil as institutional and mundane, particularly right now, but the production did not develop that idea sufficiently to make it pay off.

As a result, the stakes remained lower than they should have, and makes Tosca's decision to sacrifice herself less poignant. Scarpia's menace is the engine of Tosca: we need to believe that Tosca is trapped with a man capable of anything, and that every choice she makes carries terrible consequences. Without that sense of danger, the opera's central confrontation loses much of its power.

Brenna Corner's direction did little to compensate. Too often, the staging relied on movement without clear dramatic motivation. Characters repeatedly crossed and recrossed the stage, but the movement rarely seemed to reveal anything about their relationships, intentions, or shifting power. At times, it became distracting simply counting unnecessary stage crosses.

That is particularly damaging in _Tosca_, an opera in which physical relationships matter enormously. Who controls the action? Who is moving the pieces? Can the protagonists escape? Scarpia’s manipulation of Tosca should feel threatening and inescapable, and Tosca and Cavaradossi’s encounters should establish an intimacy that makes their sacrifices meaningful and heartbreaking. When stage movement lacks that underlying purpose, much of Puccini’s carefully constructed tension dissipates.

There was nevertheless much to admire. The production looked gorgeous, the chorus was thrilling, and the scale of Vancouver Opera's musical forces served Puccini well.
